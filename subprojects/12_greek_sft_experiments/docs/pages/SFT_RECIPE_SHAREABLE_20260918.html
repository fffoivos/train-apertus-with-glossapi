The Greek Apertus: how we did supervised fine-tuning

An 8-billion-parameter open model, continued in Greek from Apertus 8B and then instruction-tuned on a 384,010-row mixture in a single pass. This describes the data, the hyperparameters, how we arrived at them, the schedule, and where the model stands against comparable models. Figures come from the run receipts; the caveats are included rather than smoothed over.

How the Greek sets were built

All six Greek blocks go through the same four stages, and the differences between them are entirely in stage three — in how much of the row a machine can check.

1. Design before generation. Each set starts from a written plan that fixes the cells to be filled before anything is generated: for constraint following, 880 subtopics × 12 question forms × 40 constraint families at five composition levels; for mathematics, grade × topic × context × surface; for rewriting, 20 genres × 40 topics × 5 lengths × a register; for identity, seven categories over a 110-fact sheet about Greece, a 14-statement identity sheet and a positions guide for contested topics. Plans are piloted at a few hundred rows first. The pilots decided real things: authored user requests beat templated ones, so nothing is templated; the previous round's terse maths targets taught the model to answer in a median of 35 words with no boxed answer and tripled its looping rate, so cut two is worked at 100 to 250 words with the answer boxed.

2. Generation under a contract. A model writes each row from its cell, at medium effort for volume work and high effort where the content is hard. Every generator prompt carries the same prohibitions: do not add facts, do not write in mannerisms, do not refer to yourself, and only invent person names — institutions, services and procedures named in an answer must exist or be plausible in Greece. For the adapted set one test governs everything: would swapping this entity change whether the answer is true? If yes it is content and is frozen; if no it is frame and becomes Greek. American tax brackets become Greek ones; the boiling point of water does not move.

3. Verification, as far as it goes. Constraints are machine-checked — each answer is run through the checker for its own constraints, failures retried once and dropped if they fail again (94.5% pass at the pilot; 97.4% at one constraint, 66.7% at five). Mathematics is checked against a reference answer, with a blind independent solve as a second opinion; where the two disagree the row is kept and the disagreement recorded, because two solvers disagreeing is not proof the published answer is wrong. Register, voice and identity cannot be machine-checked at all, so they are read: a model screen over every row, an independent adversarial review of a sample, and the owner reading rows blind.

4. Correction, then re-verification. An editing pass reads each row against a written contract and proposes the lightest change that fixes it, and the constraint checkers are re-run after every edit — an edit that breaks a constraint is reverted (12,361 rows edited and 163 reverted in the first two constraint builds). Edits that change a number in a maths row are forbidden. A row edited beyond a fifth of its length is held for a person rather than accepted silently.

The process removes work as readily as it adds it. The long-context recall lane of the conversation suite was audited at 100 rows, 74 came back flawed, and the whole lane — 709 rows — was dropped. The habit-correction lane was dropped because it depends on masking turns the raw-message path did not honour. 1,318 logic puzzles were removed because brute force said their published answers were wrong. Corrections found after a set is finished are applied as overlays rather than by regenerating it, so the trained set stays the set that was reviewed.

Example rows from all 22 blocks — including the Greek ones described below — are in the companion document, SFT_DATASET_EXAMPLES_20260918.html, sampled from the training file itself.

How we got there

Round one settled the optimisation, on a 17,602-row Greek-only arm. We ran learning rate against epochs with checkpoints at one, two and three epochs. At 1e-5 the held-out Greek loss bottoms at epoch two, at 5e-6 it is still falling at epoch three, which is the signature of an under-trained run. Two seeds of the identical recipe gave us a noise floor to read everything else against: held-out loss ±0.008, instruction following ±0.010, maths ±0.028. Anything smaller than that is not a result.

The same round chose the base checkpoint. The averaged pretraining checkpoint beat the terminal one, which looked equal on loss but lost four noise-floors of Greek maths and carried a deficit on native Greek tasks straight through fine-tuning.

It also tested whether adapting imported data matters. Rows moved to a Greek point of view beat the same rows left as they were, on instruction following, stop behaviour, voice and blind interviews. We later narrowed that claim: it supports adapting the frame for that experiment, not a general law about language.

Round two scaled the mixture and found the identity dose. At 334k rows the broad mixture gained 6.6 points of Greek instruction following over the round-one pick. Then three arms varied only the weight of the identity set. At weight one it still answered «Ναι, είμαι το ChatGPT». At weight two it learned who it is but invented its own licence. At weight four it answered every probed fact as written. That is why the identity set is quadrupled here.

Round three found the data defects that this run fixes. Terse Greek maths targets taught the model to answer in a median of 35 words without a boxed answer, and looping rose fivefold. A dedicated correcting set taught acceptance rather than discrimination: it held against a false correction in 20 of 3,692 supervised turns while accepting 272 times, and removing it restored the model's willingness to disagree. Both findings changed the data rather than the hyperparameters.

Three maths pilots tested the fix before the full run. Same recipe, same replay, one variable: terse targets, worked solutions, or worked solutions plus the hardest level. The accuracy difference was inside the noise and the pre-declared bar was not met, so no arm was promoted. But generation behaviour separated clearly: terse targets produced 132 truncated and 82 looping answers of 500, worked solutions produced 59 and 37, matching the reference model. The worked solutions went in on that basis, and the accuracy question was left open rather than claimed.

So: the optimisation was chosen once against a measured noise floor, re-confirmed at scale, and then held fixed for three rounds while only the data and the supervision path changed.

The one change of supervision. Earlier rounds handed raw messages to the trainer, which meant per-message masks were ignored and a few tokens after multi-byte characters went unsupervised: 20 tokens in 14,062 on a sampled audit, in 16% of rows. This run is the first on the corrected path, where the labels the validator computes are the labels the trainer uses. Because masks were not yet trusted end to end, every block that depends on masking was excluded. Data and supervision changed together, so gains belong to the pass as a whole, not to any single block.

3. The run

One pass from the pretrained base, no second stage: 6 hours 39 minutes on one node of four GH200s, 6.65 node-hours, about 10,200 tokens per second. Final training loss 0.966, token accuracy rising from 0.696 to 0.752. Learning rate warms to 9.98e-6 by 6% of the epoch and decays to 1.02e-6 at the end. Checkpoints every 750 steps keeping two, plus the end of the epoch, which is the published model.

Everything is bound by hash before launch: the training file, the dev file, the trainer and the config, checked identical on the laptop and on the cluster and recorded in the receipt. Decontamination runs against 13 evaluation caches, including all 16,632 Greek MMLU items and 1,000 MATH-500 items.

Mathematics, in both directions

The Greek maths set was rebuilt for this run to fix a specific, documented failure. The previous round's terse targets had taught the model to answer in a median of 35 words with no boxed answer; it looped on about 15% of problems and truncated 96 of 500. The fix was worked solutions of 100 to 250 words with the answer boxed. On its own terms the fix worked.

The competition-maths gain is robust: 52 items the new model answers correctly that the old one missed, against 25 the other way. The grade-school loss is real but sits at the edge of significance — its interval comes within 0.4 points of zero, and the 3-point promotion tolerance it breached is narrower than the benchmark's own standard error on 250 items, about 3.2 points. We report it as a failure because the rule was frozen in advance, not because the evidence is strong.

Read these comparisons carefully.
  • Greek IFEval numbers are rescored after a language checker was found to be scoring every model zero. Older published figures for the same models are 2 to 4 points lower on the original scoring; use one column throughout.
  • Greek MMLU is the official letter protocol over all items. An earlier project figure used a decontaminated subset and a different scorer, and is about 12 points lower for everyone. The two families are not comparable.
  • Retention benchmarks are all in chat mode, which costs every model roughly a point against the base-model framing. Both sides of each comparison are chat mode.
  • MT-Bench and the Greek quality review are single-judge model scores. Useful as a signal, not for ranking.
  • Krikri is evaluated in base mode on its own suite's protocol, ours and Apertus in chat mode.
  • Apertus-Instruct's MATH score is an extraction artefact: it answers without the boxed format, so the scorer cannot read it. We report it as a diagnostic, not a ranking.

What the run did not pass

We froze promotion criteria before launching. The model met the improvement bar, held retention, and kept generation behaviour inside the reference range, but it lost 8 points of Greek MGSM against the previous model, beyond the 3-point tolerance. Under the frozen rule that is a hold, and we recorded it as one. A paired investigation on the same 250 items ruled out scoring artefacts, truncation, looping and non-Greek output: the model simply answers differently, 36 items right that the old one got wrong and 56 the other way. That is a −8.0 point difference with a 95% paired interval of [−15.6, −0.4] and an exact McNemar p of 0.047 — real, but close enough to the boundary that a rerun could land either side of it, and the 3-point tolerance it breached is itself narrower than the benchmark's standard error on 250 items. The same test applied to Greek competition maths, where the run improved, returns a much firmer +5.4 points, [+2.0, +8.8], p = 0.003. We held the model because the rule was frozen in advance; the evidence for the failing criterion is weaker than the evidence for the passing one. A generation format gate also failed at 0.86 against a 0.95 threshold for ending cleanly on the stop token.

We have no ablation of the individual data changes in this pass, because data and supervision changed together. Whether more than one worked solution per problem helps mathematics is the open question we are testing next.
