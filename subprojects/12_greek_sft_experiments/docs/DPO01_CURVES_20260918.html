A Loader Bug, and What It Hid

Preference training on 343 pairs (plus 54 held out), 204 of them labelled Greek, from one supervised parent. For a day this page reported the round as damaging — six to eight points of mathematics gone, five to eight of multilingual knowledge. It was a loading fault. The checkpoints were written by one version of the library and scored by another, which silently gave every trained model the wrong rotary base while the parent kept the right one. Re-scored correctly, with the evaluation prompt frozen so two runs are actually the same measurement, all fifteen checkpoints score above the parent on both generated benchmarks. The first half of this page is the within-run measurement that was never affected. The second half is the correction, and the several things that still do not fit.

3. What the loss cannot see

The objective only cares about the gap between the two replies. It is perfectly happy to widen that gap by making the rejected reply very unlikely, even if the good reply becomes less likely too. So the quantity to watch is not the likelihood itself but its displacement from the parent — how far each reply has moved from where the parent had it, measured on the same batch:

Δchosen(t) = log πt(yw|x) − log πref(yw|x)   ·   Δrejected(t) = log πt(yl|x) − log πref(yl|x)

Solid is chosen, dashed is rejected, nats against the frozen reference; the heavy horizontal is the parent. All four panels share one y scale.

On the pairs it trains on, plain DPO does not push the chosen reply down. Arm 01 holds chosen a few nats above the parent through epoch 3 while dragging rejected down 24 nats. Arm 03, anchored, lifts chosen about 12 nats. Neither is displacing the good answer downward on data it has seen three times.
The displacement is out of sample, and only the plain arm has it. On the 54 held-out pairs, arm 01's chosen sits 11.2 nats below the parent — it looked healthy in training and is not. Arm 03 ends +0.5 above the parent with the best reward accuracy of the four. That is the finding: at the learning rate that actually moves the margin, plain DPO buys it with out-of-sample likelihood displacement, and the anchor buys the same discrimination without paying.

4. The anchor is a dial, not a switch

Adding three arms between the endpoints turns the earlier yes/no question into a dose-response. Everything here is held fixed at lr 2e-6; only the chosen-NLL weight moves.

It behaves like a control, and that is the useful finding. Displacement falls monotonically as the anchor tightens — −11.2, −8.3, −4.8, −2.4, +0.5 nats — so the chosen-NLL term is not a binary safeguard but a tunable trade. What it trades against is the margin: the rejected reply is pulled up alongside the chosen one, from −15.8 nats to −0.6.
No intermediate setting escapes both problems. The pre-registered rule was to take the smallest alpha that removes the displacement (chosen within 1 nat of the parent). Only alpha 1.0 qualifies — and that is also the setting that memorises hardest, lifting chosen likelihood +14.5 nats on the pairs it trains on while returning +0.5 on pairs it has not seen. Alpha 0.5 still leaves −2.4 nats. The dial does not have a free position on it.

6. The correction, and the result

The checkpoints were loaded wrongly for a whole day of measurement. They were saved by transformers 5.16, which records the rotary-position settings under a new key. The environment that runs our generated benchmarks uses transformers 4.57, which does not read that key and falls back to a default: every trained model ran with a rotary base of 12,000,000 instead of the intended 500,000, while the parent — saved by the older version — kept the right one. We measured the cost directly by mis-loading the parent's own weights the same way: −4.07 pp IFEval, −9.60 pp MGSM, −7.63 pp Global-MMLU-Lite. That is most of the "damage" this page reported, and on IFEval several times it. It does not exceed every figure: the worst reported MGSM deficit was −9.8 pp against an artefact of −9.60, and the worst multilingual −8.4 against −7.63.

So everything below was re-measured: nineteen runs with the prompt date frozen (section 7), the rotary settings restored, and each model's effective geometry checked against the parent's before it was scored. Comparisons are made through a guard that refuses any pair differing in more than the one declared variable, so a cell in this table exists only if the prompts, the effective generation settings, the item set, the gold answers, the tokenizer and the model geometry were all identical and the weights were the only difference.

Four things this does not say, each of which we got wrong before.
· It is not "maths-free tuning improved maths". 24 dedicated mathematics tasks were excluded from the pair set, but 66 pairs keep embedded quantitative content (56 training, 10 held out) — prices, wages, schedules, quantities, explicit calculations. This page said "no mathematics" repeatedly. That was wrong.
· The IFEval gain concentrates where answers were being cut off. Splitting one arm's items by whether either answer hit the 1,280-token cap: where neither did, +1.98 pp (p=0.34); where one did, +13.79 pp. 57% of the net improvement sits in the cap-affected stratum of 87 items; the parent hits the cap on 81 of 541, the arm on 26. Termination behaviour is a plausible contributor, not a measured half: stratum membership depends on the outputs themselves, this is one checkpoint, and stopping at the right point can itself be what an instruction asked for.
· Greek knowledge went the other way. Under the official GreekMMLU protocol the arms are 0.26 to 0.43 pp below the parent, and those are the most statistically solid numbers on this page (p ≤ 0.0065 on 16,632 items). See section 10.
· The last row never used IPO. The trainer hardcoded the sigmoid objective, so a configuration asking for IPO trained sigmoid anyway and said nothing. Those two runs are anchored sigmoid DPO at β=10. Every IPO conclusion this page carried — a pre-registered falsification, a gate that went unmet, an argument about β scaling the gradient — was about a loss function that never ran.

MGSM is the one that survives scrutiny best. Response lengths are unchanged (121.1 tokens for both parent and plain arm), the gained answers are longer rather than shorter, in only 4 of 48 gains did the parent's text hold the right number anywhere — four possible extraction cases, not four proven ones, and the items that change replicate across runs (gain-set Jaccard 0.646 / 0.702 / 0.717 for the plain, anchored and balanced groups; loss-set 0.594 / 0.609 / 0.662, and lower again at 0.533 / 0.375 for the β=10 pair). It is a real change in the answers. What produces it is unknown, and 79 of 250 items move to net +17, which is a great deal of churn for a set of 343 training pairs.

Verification status. Thirteen independent cross-vendor reviews (gpt-5.6-sol, gpt-6-astra) have now examined this work. The most recent re-derived the training-time geometry, confirmed all fifteen arms resolve to distinct intended weight hashes, checked 3,191 benchmark documents for contamination against the pair set at 8, 13 and 20 tokens (none), and re-ran 540 Global-MMLU comparisons. Its verdict on the result was real, with three corrections — the four bullets above are theirs, not ours. What is still not established: the mechanism behind the MGSM gain, and whether any of this transfers beyond these protocols.

7. The 2×2 we built to settle this is confounded by the date

transformers 5.16.1 re-serialised every checkpoint with a different eos_token_id and use_cache off, so the 18 September page was comparing re-serialised checkpoints against the parent as originally served. Scoring the parent's own weights under a checkpoint's config appeared to cost 1.7 pp IFEval and 2.4 pp MGSM, and that was called a correction. It cannot have been a configuration effect: lm_eval forces the cache on regardless, and the two end-of-sequence lists ([2, 68] and [68, 2, 68]) stop on exactly the same tokens, so after every override the effective generation settings of parent and checkpoints are byte-identical. Those two parent runs differed in one thing, the prompt date. On 19 September we measured the missing fourth cell — an arm's weights under the parent's config — and read the result as a weights×config interaction that reversed the sign of the IFEval effect.

That reading does not survive. The cells were run on different days, and the evaluation prompt contains the date. Every IFEval prompt carries a Current date: line, and it differs in 541 of 541 prompts in each affected comparison — 250 of 250 for MGSM. The pattern is exactly crossed:

weightsunder the parent's configunder the checkpoint's config
parent335/541 · dated 18 Sept326/541 · dated 19 Sept
plain α=0321/541 · dated 19 Sept334/541 · dated 18 Sept
anchored α=0.25326/541 · dated 19 Sept336/541 · dated 18 Sept

The date is anti-correlated with the configuration across the two columns, so every contrast changes the weights and the prompt text. Normalising the date leaves zero other differences between the prompts, and the generation arguments and benchmark documents match — which is what makes it a clean confound rather than a vague worry. The differences-in-differences, 3.51 and 4.07 pp, cannot be assigned to configuration.

What can still be said. The recorded conditions produce opposite IFEval contrasts depending on which column you read, and a changing system-prompt date prevents identifying either a configuration interaction or a training effect under either configuration. The 1.7 pp and 2.4 pp “pure configuration” penalties are qualified the same way, because those comparisons also change the date. Every score here is retained; it is the causal reading that is withdrawn. That re-run has since been done, with the prompt frozen and the loading fault repaired; its results are in section 6 and they supersede every arm figure below.

The confound is not confined to these six cells. Standard-arm seeds 42–43 were scored on 18 September and seeds 44–46 on 19 September; the balanced and IPO runs are all 19 September. The Global-MMLU-Lite prompts carry the date too — scoring by likelihood with no generation removes the sampling, not the prompt text. Found by an independent conclusions review (R-DPO7c); we had not looked at the rendered prompts at all.

9. Three different uncertainties, previously called one thing

Two of the three numbers in this section are contaminated by the prompt date. Each five-seed group mixes runs scored on 18 September (seeds 42–43) and 19 September (seeds 44–46), so the “between-seed SD” contains a date effect. And the “same-seed repeat” pair was scored a day apart, so its gap is date plus non-determinism, inseparably. The distinction the section draws still holds and the item-sampling column is unaffected; the other two columns need a frozen-date, correctly loaded re-score before they mean anything — and even then the five-seed groups will not be pure seed replicates, because seeds 42–43 and 44–46 were trained with different dates in their prompts, which no re-score can undo.

The earlier page used the phrase “the noise floor” for whichever of these was nearest to hand, and at one point compared a mean taken against one baseline with a floor measured against another. They are not interchangeable:

benchmarkitemsbetween-seed SD (n=5)one same-seed repeatitem sampling SE, one run
Greek IFEval5410.76 pp-0.9 pp2.10 pp
Greek MGSM2502.30 pp-2.8 pp3.04 pp
GreekMMLU2500.18 pp—3.10 pp

Between-seed SD is how much the answer changes if you retrain with a different seed. The same-seed repeat is a single contrast between two runs with identical config and seed, whose training diverged from optimiser step 3 (they share 3 of 129 loss values; non-deterministic reduction order under ZeRO-3 is the likely but unproven cause) and which were scored a day apart, so their gap mixes training divergence with the prompt date. It is one number, not a distribution. Item sampling SE is how much the score would move on a fresh draw of items from the same pool, and on these benchmark sizes it is the largest of the three.

The item term dominates, and it was never in the picture. On GreekMMLU the between-seed SD is 0.18 pp and the item SE is 3.10 pp — seventeen times larger. Quoting the 0.18 as the precision of a 2 pp gain, which is what the 18 September page did, understates the uncertainty by more than an order of magnitude. Seed stability is not measurement precision.

For a comparison on a fixed slice the item SE is the wrong term too, since both models answer the same items; the right one is the uncertainty over items that actually disagree. That is the next section, and it is where the GreekMMLU result dies.

10. The GreekMMLU gain is not a gain

Every arm scored about 0.595 on GreekMMLU-250 against the parent's 0.576, with a between-seed SD of 0.002. That looked like the one clean positive result on the page. Both models answer the identical 250 items, so the honest test is paired: how many items went wrong to right, how many went right to wrong.

About nine items are gained and four lost, in every group. The two-sided exact test over discordant pairs gives p between 0.15 and 0.42 — no group separates from the parent. The last row is where this gets complicated.

The IPO arm gets the same gain, and that is suggestive rather than decisive. It ties the other two at +5 net items. We previously called it “the arm that did not train” and built the withdrawal on that. It is not. Its trainer state records 129 of 129 optimiser steps at epoch 3.0, the same as every other arm; the 2% figure is how far its dev separation got towards a pre-registered target, which is a statement about generalisation, not about how much training happened. Its objective also carries the same α=0.25 anchor. So the right reading is that three differently trained checkpoints moved the same items — which does not isolate a preference-specific cause, and does not establish the absence of one either.

The slice explains why. Across the parent and fifteen trained runs, 235 of the 250 items never change at all: 139 everyone gets right, 96 everyone gets wrong. The entire result lives in the 15 items that move, and within those there is a shared core of 8 items that flip right and 3 that flip wrong in every single arm, the IPO arm included — the same item IDs, not merely the same counts. Individual runs add a flip or two of their own (union 10 gained / 5 lost across 15 runs, 5 distinct signatures; the IPO arm is 10 gained / 5 lost where a typical plain run is 9 / 4), but no arm escapes that core.

This also explains the 0.002. The between-seed SD was small because almost nothing on this slice can move, not because the measurement is precise. A tiny seed SD here reflects how similarly these particular models answer these particular items; it is neither evidence that a difference is real nor proof that the benchmark could not register one. What is withdrawn is the interpretation, not the measurement. Those three checkpoints really do answer 149 of 250 against the parent's 144. What that cannot support is “+2 pp of Greek knowledge”: the figure is specific to this slice, and the full benchmark below shows it shrinking to about a quarter of its size.

Then we ran the whole benchmark

So the full run settles the headline and opens a smaller question. The +2 pp figure was a property of that particular 250 items. A much smaller positive difference is observed on items no claim had been made on; paired over the 16,382 held-out items:

The clean-subset population gives the same direction on 15,916 items (+0.46 / +0.38 / +0.55). The honest statement is that the trained checkpoints score about 0.3 to 0.5 pp higher than the parent on previously unexamined custom-full-text items (all seven scored arms, table above; the paired tests here cover the first three) — with every arm mis-loaded by that runner (section 6), so this is not an improvement attributable to the weights, and that the mechanism and the preference-specificity of that improvement are unresolved. We do not claim it is caused by preference learning, and — correcting an earlier version of this page — we do not claim it is not: no untrained round-trip or arbitrary-perturbation control was ever measured, so "any perturbation would do this" was an assertion, not a finding.

And then under the official protocol, where the sign reverses

Everything above in this section was scored by a custom protocol — the full text of each choice ranked by mean token log-probability — which puts the parent 15 points below its official GreekMMLU score. A result from a different ruler is not worth much, so the same four models were re-scored on the same 16,632 items under the official protocol: the four labels Α/Β/Γ/Δ ranked by summed log-probability, no chat template. One run, one day, no date in the prompt, one dataset hash, tokenizers verified byte-identical — the weights are the only thing that differs.

The official protocol is also the quieter instrument: about 330 items change answer under it against about 1,130 under the custom one.

So the GreekMMLU story on this page has now been wrong three ways: a +2 pp gain read off a lucky slice, then a withdrawal argued from a false premise, then a small positive effect measured with the wrong ruler. The supportable statement is that the three trained checkpoints evaluated score 0.26 to 0.43 points below the parent under official-label GreekMMLU — and after the re-score it is no longer the only correctly loaded contrast, but it remains the one that points downward. These are item-level tests on one checkpoint per recipe, so they establish the difference for these models, not its reproducibility across training seeds.

Why the two results disagree in sign is not established, and they differ in more than protocol: the custom runner used the environment that mis-loads the arms' rotary settings (section 6) and the official one did not. That is a concrete, untested candidate. Another, equally untested: preference training shifts the likelihood of fluent, well-formed text, which the custom protocol reads directly off the choice text and the label protocol does not see. That is a hypothesis; nothing here tests it.

The custom-protocol numbers above are not official GreekMMLU numbers. The runner ranks the full text of each choice by mean token log-probability — a custom protocol. The same parent scores about 69.4% under the official label protocol against 54.2% here, a 15-point gap. Every custom-protocol GreekMMLU figure on this page is comparable only with other custom-protocol figures here — and, since section 6, only arm against arm: that runner used the environment that mis-loads the arms. The official-protocol figures above are the ones comparable with a published score. The clean-subset figures (16,159 items, 473 contaminated items excluded under the frozen manifest) move none of the conclusions: +0.41 to +0.58.

At the time this was written it removed the only positive knowledge result; the corrected Global-MMLU-Lite figures in section 6 are positive for three groups of four. The multilingual scores looked lower too (5.3 to 8.4 points down) — but every one of those arm runs was mis-loaded. Corrected, the same lane is up 1.10 to 1.77 points for three groups and flat (−0.19) for the balanced subset. Per-item predictions for all 66 scored models are in results/G4F6P1--DPO01/greekmmlu_items/.

11. The length-balanced subset works, for reasons we did not isolate

MGSM correlates with how much shorter the chosen replies are than the rejected ones, so we rebuilt the pair set to remove that skew: greedy matching on the word-count difference, keeping 274 of the 343 training pairs (the 54 dev pairs are separate), bringing the chosen/rejected mean word ratio from 0.785 to 0.992 and the share of pairs where chosen is shorter from 59% to 50%. Three seeds.

The MGSM deficit falls from about −6.3 pp to −1.6 pp, and instruction following is descriptively similar (which is not the same as demonstrated equivalence). This is the largest effect on the page and it survives the date check: against 19 September standard runs the balanced arm is +6.00 pp over the plain arm and +4.53 pp over the anchored one, and every balanced run (40.0–41.6%) beats every standard-arm run (32.4–38.4%). It is worth pursuing.

It is not a length experiment. Selecting 274 of the 343 training pairs changes three things simultaneously: the length distribution, which examples are in the set, and the amount of training — 105 optimiser steps against 129, about 19% less. Selection can shift task mix, difficulty and pair quality along with length, and matching on words does not match on model tokens. The honest statement is that the selected subset reduced the observed MGSM deficit; length, selection and exposure remain confounded.
Corrected, the trade is smaller and the arm is not worse than the parent. Against the other arms the balanced subset is +2.72 pp MGSM over plain and +2.16 over anchored, while sitting 1.29 and 1.69 pp lower on Global-MMLU-Lite — not the roughly +5 / −3 this page reported from the mis-loaded runs. Against the parent it is the best arm on both generated lanes (+5.48 IFEval, +11.20 MGSM) and essentially flat on multilingual (−0.19). The trade-off is real but modest, and length, example selection and training exposure remain confounded, so it is a promising recipe rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

It sits awkwardly with the exposure story — the earlier page argued damage recedes as training goes on, and this arm trained less and was damaged less — but that is not a refutation: a different subset improving with fewer steps and a fixed dataset improving with more steps are different comparisons, changing different things. The exposure claim is withdrawn above on its own evidence, not because of this arm.

Separating the three would take a matched control: the same 274 pairs re-rendered to restore the original length skew, or 274 pairs sampled at random from the 343 training pairs to hold count constant while leaving length alone. Neither was run. Until one is, this is a promising recipe rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

12. The IPO test was never run

The objective in the configuration was ignored. These two runs were configured with loss_type: ipo, but the trainer passed loss_type="sigmoid" to TRL as a literal, so the setting was overridden without a warning. They are anchored sigmoid DPO at β=10 with the same α=0.25 anchor as arm 05. Everything this page previously said about them — a pre-registered falsification, a dev-separation target of 0.0500 reached only to 0.0010, an argument about β scaling the initial gradient by 1/β — described a loss function that never ran. The correct statement is simply: the intended IPO test was not executed.

Their scores are real for the weights that exist. As a β=10 sigmoid pair they are the weakest arm on the generated lanes (+1.94 IFEval, +5.00 MGSM against the parent), which is what a very high β would predict — it scales the effective learning signal down. That is an observation about β, not about IPO.

The displacement hypothesis those runs were meant to falsify therefore remains untested. The trainer now reads the objective from the configuration and refuses an unsupported value, so the test can actually be run; it costs two training runs.

Found by an independent review (R-DPO13) reading the deployed trainer against the configuration files. loss_type was the only configuration key the trainer overrode, so the fault is confined to these two runs.

13. What would settle any of this

The pattern across sections 6 to 12 is the same one every time: a result measured once, on an aggregate score, against a baseline that differed in more than the thing being tested. The replication did not overturn the measurements — every number from 18 September reproduced — it overturned the readings of them.

  • Explain the mathematics gain, or stop calling it one. 79 of 250 items move for a net +17. A held-out quantitative benchmark, pre-registered, would say whether this is capability or protocol. Until then "MGSM exact match increased" is the whole claim.
  • Finish the official GreekMMLU matrix. Four models of nineteen are scored under the protocol that disagrees. The remaining twelve are running.
  • Re-run the IPO arm, as IPO. The trainer now honours the objective in the config. The falsification test this round thought it had run has still not been run.
  • Separate brevity from compliance on IFEval. The cap-split above does it crudely; a length-controlled scoring pass would do it properly.
  • One evaluation environment. The whole fault was two versions of one library disagreeing about a config key. Training and evaluation should resolve model geometry identically, and the manifest should record it either way — which it now does.
On the round as a whole. DPO round 1 worked, and a loading fault spent a day disguising it as damage. Fifteen checkpoints score above the supervised parent on both generated benchmarks, by group means of +1.94 to +5.48 points on instruction following and +5.00 to +11.20 on mathematics (individual checkpoints span +1.48 to +5.91 and +4.40 to +13.20), under a comparison where the prompts, the generation settings, the items, the tokenizer and the model geometry were all verified identical. That is the finding. It comes with three qualifications that are not decoration: most of the instruction-following improvement is concentrated in items where an answer was being cut off, so how much of it is compliance rather than termination is unresolved; Greek knowledge measured under its own protocol went down on the three checkpoints scored so far, with the firmest statistics on the page; and nobody can yet say why mathematics moved. The round is a success and an unexplained one, which is a better position than it looked like this morning and a worse one than the top line suggests.

14. How to read this if you know the DPO paper

The gradient of the plain objective pushes up the chosen log-probability and down the rejected one, weighted by how wrong the implicit reward currently is. Nothing in it fixes the absolute level, so a run can satisfy the objective by sliding both down. That is the failure the DPO-Positive paper (Pal et al., 2402.13228) names. The anchor used here is of the same family — a per-example negative log-likelihood term on the chosen answer, so the good answer has to stay probable — but it is our own term with its own weight, not that paper's method, and nothing here reproduces or tests their result.

What this run adds is where to look for the failure. Measured on the training pairs, plain DPO at 2e-6 looks innocent — chosen stays at or above the parent for all three epochs. The displacement appears only on pairs the arm has never seen, at −11.2 nats. A diagnostic that reads the training curves alone would have passed arm 01.

It is tempting to go further and say the anchor was therefore pointless, and an earlier version of this page did: alpha controlled displacement monotonically while the benchmark comparison stayed unresolved. On the mis-loaded runs that contrast was +0.70 pp; corrected, it is −0.07 pp with a seed-paired interval of [−0.52, +0.37] — closer to zero than before, and still not separated. That argument does not work. An interval crossing zero leaves the effect unresolved; it does not demonstrate equivalence. And the replicated contrast does not test the hypothesis: alpha 0.25 still leaves −4.77 nats of held-out displacement, and the setting that actually removes it is alpha 1.0 at +0.49 nats — which was never part of the five-seed comparison. So this round does not establish that removing displacement fails to help, nor that displacement was not a binding constraint. The ranking stays withdrawn; the question stays open, and it is a cheap one to answer properly.
